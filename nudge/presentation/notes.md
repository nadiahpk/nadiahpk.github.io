# Title slide (0:30)

Students miss out on in-person workshops for all kinds of reasons, including work and caring responsibilities.
It's not enough to show them a worked solution because that doesn't provide the *productive struggle* 
that will deepend their understanding and build self-efficacy.

Point-and-click adventure games (gesture) do productive struggle well: they let you try and fail,
they nudge you forward without handing the answer to you.
They make you want to persist.

# 1a. What did we do? (1:00)

We built two interactive linear algebra worksheets: two weeks, two multipart questions.

The key design feature is branching paths. The student decides where to go next.

Hints are nested, to reveal gradually.

And when the student gets the answer wrong ---
I don't know how to do Gaussian elimination ---
the response isn't "Wrong, try again", it's "We're going to learn this together".

# 2a. Who did we reach? (1:00)

This semester, marks improved on every question, all significant.

Now we should be careful because the questions *are* different,

## Substantive errors (1:30)

The rest were dominated by conceptual errors and missing explanations.

Now I can't rule out a cohort effect,
but there's two reasons I'm hopeful.

First, the specificity of the response.
Q2c was about marking the right-angle between projection and error vectors.
I saw some confused diagrams, but-- they had a right-angle symbol in there.

Targetting missing explanations is easy, but we've got a counterfactual as well.

For Q1d, there was a particular trick I wanted them to use,
but I received feedback that part of the worksheet was confusing.
And I could see it in the trace data as well-- I did a bad job on that one.
And the marks reflected that -- no significant change in that behaviour.

So I'm cautiously optimistic that the interactive worksheet was the cause of improved outcomes,
and the engagement data shows it reached the cohort I wanted.

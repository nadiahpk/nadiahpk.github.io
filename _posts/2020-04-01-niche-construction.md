**Niche construction is a framework we could use to think about sustainable resource use**

Niche construction is a framework that we could use to think about sustainable resource use.
Niche construction refers to the modification of the selective environment by organisms; 
for example, the building of nests, 
which might lead to selection for their maintenance and defence (Laland et al., 2016). 
In cases where the niche construction influences the environment for the constructor’s
offspring, niche construction adds an ecological inheritance to the descendants’ genetic inheritance
(Kendal et al., 2011). 
Consequently, the niche-construction perspective entails reciprocal causation:
a feedback between organisms driving environmental change and modified environments selecting
for changes in organisms (Laland et al., 2016).

There is a growing body of evidence for gene-culture interactions shaping human evolution, for
example, the coevolution of dairy farming and adult lactose tolerance (Laland et al., 2010). 
In humans, 
ecological inheritance is particularly powerful because it includes semantic information (i.e.,
information that reduces uncertainty about the environment) such as teachings from elders, 
along with material changes such as the effect of past practices on current resource availability 
and quality (Kendal et al., 2011). 
Therefore, we may expect, for example, that inter-generational harvesting of the same 
resource territory may create selection for defence of resource territories, maintanence of the
resource, and practices and cultural beliefs that promote its continued abundance (see kin selection
below).

**Intergenerational wealth transfer can promote the sustainable use of that wealth**

When a resource can be privatised and transferred directly to one’s offspring, 
then it can be worthwhile to forego immediate exploitation of that resource 
in favour of preserving its quality for continued sustainable use 
(McNamara and Houston, 2006; Shennan, 2011). This is because the fitness
of a resource-use strategy is not measured by counting one’s offspring, but also the number of their
offspring and other descendants far into the future. 
Therefore, although a resource-conserving strategy will lead to fewer offspring in the short term, 
if the continued quality of that resource has even
a modest effect on the number of offspring in each generation, 
then the strategy will have higher fitness in the long term (McNamara and Houston, 2006).

**Intergenerational kin selection for sustainable resource use**

The situation is more complicated when the resource is not privately owned but communely shared;
however, the principle remains, that foregoing immediate use can be beneficial in the long term.

In theoretical models, a niche-construction trait that is costly to the focal individual 
--- but enhances
the fitness of individuals residing in that environment --- 
can evolve if the direct fitness costs are outweighed by future indirect benefits (Lehmann, 2007, 2008). 
Two conditions are required for this to occur. 
First, the effect of the trait on the environment must persist in time. 
Second, dispersal must be limited 
so that future individuals in that environment are likely to have inherited the actor’s genes
(Lehmann, 2008). In particular, this mechanism can be used to model the evolution of sustainable
use of a common resource pool (Lehmann, 2007). In essence, it is a kin-selection mechanism for the
evolution of altruism, but the kin who benefit are relatives who live in the future.

**Dispersal and the evolution of cooperation**

It is interesting to note that the Lehmann models above circumvent the long-known theoretical result
that kin selection cannot promote altruism by limited dispersal alone. From Hamilton’s kin selection
theory, we might expect that, because limited dispersal increases relatedness, then it should also
favour the evolution of altruism. However, limited dispersal also increases the relatedness between
competitors, which opposes selection for altruism (reviewed in West et al., 2002). As a consequence,
in models on both patch-structured and lattice-structured landscapes, and when the population size
is at carrying capacity and fixed, these two effects ‘cancel out’ (Taylor, 1992b,a). This is result is
general and applies to all areas where kin selection theory can be applied (West et al., 2002). A similar
result is also obtained for cooperation in groups of non-relatives (West et al., 2006): 
local competition
reduces selection for cooperation because the beneficiary is also the provider’s competitor.

There are, however, biological details that can circumvent the ‘cancelling’ effect described above.
For example, budding dispersal reduces local competition by exporting the additional progeny that
altruism creates while maintaining the relatedness between interacting individuals (Gardner and
West, 2006). In short, the problem is circumvented when relatedness can be increased locally while
competition is ‘exported’ and occurs more globally (Van Baalen and Rand, 1998; West et al., 2002).
Lehmann (2007) writes that his niche-construction model achieves this because, by helping relatives
that live in the future, 
the helper is not helping individuals that are in direct competition with themselves. 
He also notes that the kin structure in his model is similar to that of models with overlapping
generations. 
Models with overlapping generations (Taylor and Irwin, 2000) circumvent the cancelling by “increasing relatedness between altruists and beneficiaries, but not the probability that
relatives will compete” (West et al., 2002).

**Examples of sustainable use of shared resources by hunter-gatherers**

Hunter-gatherer groups provide many examples of sustainable management of wild / semi-wild
shared resources. Culturally-modified trees provide an example of intentional resource management, 
where harvesting of wood for tools or removal of bark to retrieve cambium for food was done
in such a way as to keep the tree alive (Turner et al., 2009). 
Other examples include long-term management of estuarine gardens, 
crabapple orchards (Turner and Jones, 2000), root-vegetable growing
and berry-picking areas, where selective harvesting, weeding, replanting, etc. were practiced on
wild / semi-wild resources (Deur, 2009). Clam gardens provide an example of long-term cultural
investment over multiple generations (Groesbeck et al., 2014).

**Ownership is important for sustainable use of resources**

Ownership of resource territories, either by family groups or clans, 
plays an important role in management of shared resources (Turner and Jones, 2000). 
There are many examples where population
decline has occurred because the tenure over communal resources has been weakened, leading to
poaching, overharvesting, and other mismanagements (e.g. wild medicinal roots in Ait M’hamed
Valley, Morocco Ouarghidi et al., 2017)

In hunter-gatherer societies, 
wild resources are often demarcated to indicate ownership by individuals or families (Deur, 2000). 
For example, wild huckleberry patches were divided into discrete territories for harvesting by each tribe and harvest-time capmpsites were inherited matrilineally (Deur,
2009); crabapple trees were marked with wooden stakes to indicate a family’s ownership (Turner
and Turner, 2008); and clam gardens were maintained over multiple generations (Groesbeck et al.,
2014).

**Hunter-gatherer resource management as a case study of the intergenerational kin-selection mechanism?**

A group’s population size will be limited by its its ability to both 
harvest and promote the continued abundance of a food resource (Turner and Jones, 2000), therefore inter-group competition and
kin selection may be the driving force behind communal resource management. Clam gardens, for
example, respresent a substantial investment in infrastructure, which can be understood in terms of
niche-construction and ecological inheritance (see above). Younger family members will inherit both
the physical clam gardens and the knowledge of their use (Moss and Wellman, 2017). Therefore, it is
in the current generation’s interests to sustain these resources and to pass on the knowledge of how
to sustain them into the future.

**Hunter-gatherer motivations for sustainable resource use**

Practices that promote the sustainable use of these resources may be motivated by practical concerns
(Turner et al., 2009) and/or encoded in a moral framework (Deur, 2009). 
For example, in the Klamath and Modoc traditions (Oregon, USA), selective harvesting of trees is understood in terms of an
interspecific ethics that recognises reciprocal obligations between humans and non-human animals
and plants, and the scattering of propagules was understood as an offering back to nature (Deur,
2009).
Interestingly, although ‘sustainability’ 
--- in the sense of preserving a resource for future generations --- is cited as a rule of thumb for guiding actions (Turner and Jones, 2000), some traditions are critical
of this way of thinking. Sustainability is viewed as another way of continuing to ‘take’ from the land,
in contrast to the preferred mindset of a human-nature reciprocal relationship and and an attitude
of gratitude, which is theorised to induce self-restraint (Kimmerer, 2011).

**Psychology and conservation**

Conservation psychology is an emerging, mission-oriented field, 
which is concerned with understanding people’s psychological relationships with nature and how to encourage people to take care
of the natural world (Saunders, 2003). The field has a particular interest in social dilemmas, broadly
defined as when short term self interests are at odds with longer term collective interests (Van Lange
et al., 2013). The theory is complementary to the game theory above; while a model like Lehmann
(2007) deals with ultimate evolutionary causes of cooperative behaviour, models in this field focus
on proximate psychological causes like the effect that the composition of personality types will have
on cooperation outcomes (e.g. Sircova et al., 2015). Studies in this area often ask questions about po-
litical orientations, worldviews, and emotional responses of survey participants to the conservation
problem (Leviston et al., 2015, e.g.).
